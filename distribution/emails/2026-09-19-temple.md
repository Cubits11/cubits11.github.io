## 1 — Eduard Dragut · `edragut@temple.edu`

**Subject:** SciER: the one integer three extractors can't give you

Dr. Dragut,

I'm an independent researcher in Philadelphia, working on when a stack of
classifiers can be evaluated from per-classifier scores alone. The short
answer is usually not, and the gap is wider than people expect.

SciER is the cleanest example I've found outside my own domain. At commit
`db347813`, `test.jsonl` holds 2,948 entity mentions across 854 sentences;
`test_ood.jsonl` holds 1,295 more. The paper reports PURE, PL-Marker and
HGERE on those splits. From three F1 scores, the number of mentions that all
three missed is bounded but not determined — and that number is what says how
much headroom an ensemble of them actually has.

Were the per-item predictions retained? Three id-keyed files would make it
exact, spans only, no scores or weights. If re-running isn't worth it, one
integer answers it: of the 2,948 mentions in `test.jsonl`, how many were
recalled by none of the three?

A script that downloads both splits at that commit, checks their SHA-256 and
recomputes the counts:
https://github.com/Cubits11/cubits11.github.io/blob/723ec1bd0851ed89e08420362c0482c2e61507d1/scripts/reanalyze_scier_testset.py

I report those counts only as counts on the released files, not as an
evaluation of any extractor. If I've misread what the repository publishes, a
correction is just as useful to me.

**Subject:** Proving a run matched its own preregistration — a conformance question

Dr. Wang,

I'm an independent researcher in Philadelphia. I've hit a problem that I think
is squarely a formal-methods problem, and I'd value ten minutes of your
scepticism on whether it's the one I think it is.

I run small empirical experiments under a preregistration: a machine-readable
contract fixes the pool, the thresholds and the estimator before any outcome
is visible. I have a validator that checks the contract's internal
consistency — structure, threshold direction, declared pool identities. What I
do not have is any proof that the runner *actually consumed* that contract.
The specification says X; nothing establishes that the execution did X. Right
now that gap is filled by my own good behaviour, which is exactly the thing a
preregistration is supposed to stop relying on.

That looks to me like refinement checking: a declared specification, an
execution trace, and an obligation that the second satisfies the first. Your
declarative network verification work solves the structurally similar problem
for routing protocols, where logic-based axioms capture semantics and a proof
discharges the property.

The question I can't answer alone: is expressing an experiment contract as a
logical specification and discharging runtime conformance as a proof
obligation over the trace a sound reduction, or am I reaching for a hammer
that doesn't fit? A blunt "this is the wrong framing, and here's why" would
genuinely save me months.

## 4 — Xinghua (Mindy) Shi · `mindyshi@temple.edu`

**Subject:** When a multi-marker panel's joint false-negative rate isn't the product of the marginals

Dr. Shi,

I'm an independent researcher in Philadelphia working on a narrow statistical
question: when several imperfect detectors are combined, what does knowing
each one's individual error rate tell you about the combination's error rate?

The answer is less than people assume. If two detectors each miss 10%, the
share of cases both miss can be anywhere from 0% to 10% depending entirely on
how their failures correlate. Multiplying to 1% doesn't estimate that
quantity — it selects one point in the feasible range, and the choice is an
assumption rather than a measurement.

I've been testing this on safety classifiers, but the structure isn't specific
to them, and multi-marker diagnostic panels seem to me the place where the
same error has the highest cost. A panel built from several noisy biomarkers
has a joint false-negative rate that is not the product of the per-marker
rates, and the difference is largest exactly when the markers share a
biological failure mode — which is when they're most likely to have been
selected together.

Is this a known and handled issue in the panel-design literature, handled
under a name I haven't found? Or is it as under-addressed as it looks from
outside? I'd rather be told it's solved than keep rediscovering it.

Pranav Bhave
Philadelphia · https://cubits11.github.io
